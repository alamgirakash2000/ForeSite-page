Abstract

On construction sites, a humanoid carrying materials may need to duck beneath an obstruction, wait for a worker, or adjust its movement around equipment. ForeSite adds a planner to a perceptive walking policy to select walking speed and body posture using postures tested during loaded walking. We evaluate a Unitree G1 robot carrying a 4.1 kg box in simulation with known hazard trajectories.

Across 120 encounters involving a crane load, a worker carrying a pipe, and an excavator arm, ForeSite records 4 hits, compared with 17 for stop-and-wait and 83 without avoidance. However, it falls in 5 of 200 runs, compared with 1 without avoidance. In separate contact simulations, harmful outcomes occur in 3 of 80 runs, compared with 9 for stop-and-wait and 31 without avoidance. Waiting outside hazard zones prevents hits but increases travel time.

We also introduce a full-scale simulated construction-site testbed with 15 hazards for evaluating humanoid material transport.

One box, one route, a different answer for each hazard

The robot carries a 4.1 kg box for 44 m. Some hazards it passes with a posture change. For others, the right move is to wait, or to keep walking.

It first has to get across the site

The walking policy RPL sees the ground through two depth cameras. ForeSite only sends it the commands it already accepts.

Fixed hazards: change posture, keep walking

A fixed object never moves out of the way. The robot squats, twists or bends by as much as the hazard needs.

Moving hazards: pick the moment

Every 0.2 s the planner looks 4 s ahead and pairs each speed plan with each posture it has measured on the walking robot.

Plug in a policy

  • Every 20 ms a policy may set forward speed, pelvis height, waist yaw and waist pitch.
  • It may know each hazard's motion model, or only noisy, late or short-range observations.
  • The testbed scores hits on 15 body spheres, falls, delivery and the outcome of every contact.
  • A run is repeatable from its seed, and it can be saved and rendered later.

How ForeSite decides

  1. Know what the body can really do

    A posture map stores where the body ends up when the loaded robot walks in each of 65 postures. While walking, it bends only about half as far as in stance when it also squats.

  2. Pair every speed plan with every posture

    Go, slow down, stop or speed up, times every posture the current surface allows. A pair survives if the predicted gap to every hazard stays at least 12 cm for the next 4 s. The cheapest one wins.

  3. Filter, then walk

    A safety filter from SPARK adjusts waist yaw, waist pitch and pelvis height toward 10 cm gaps. The walking policy RPL turns the commands into 29 joint targets at 50 Hz.
